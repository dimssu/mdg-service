Architecture note · SDMS portal automation · 5 Sep 2026

One login,
many services

Water ingress logs into the oil-company portal every two hours. The next service wants the same rhythm. Can they share one login — and what breaks if they do?

The short answer

Read this and you can stop

Verdict

Yes — but the prize is not fewer logins. Clubbing saves about 2 logins out of 14 a day per pump. The other 12 are the water check itself, and no amount of clubbing removes those.

The real prize is that the fifth service becomes cheap to write. Right now every new portal service copies the same 35 lines of "open browser, take the lock, log in, close browser". That copy-paste is the thing worth deleting.

So: build the shared login object first, and treat batching as a separate, later, smaller decision. Two facts have to be checked in a live browser before batching is even designable.

Where the logins actually go

One pump, one day, all four portal services

  • Water ingress — every 2 hours, odd hour. Writes to the portal. 12.00
  • TT density — daily, 07:35. 1.00
  • Credit & DOD — daily. 1.00
  • Inspection reports — weekly. 0.14

Twelve of those fourteen are the water check, and they are fixed by law of the portal: a slot can only be ticked while the clock is inside it, and there is no back-fill. Clubbing can only ever fold the other 2.14 into windows that were opening anyway.

Two facts nobody has checked

Both are written in code comments and proved nowhere · one afternoon, no code

BlockerDoes one login serve one pump, or several?

Several pumps already share one portal username. If one login can reach several outlets, batching across pumps is possible — and dangerous, because nothing in the system checks that the page on screen belongs to the pump being processed. Today that is safe only because each run logs in fresh with that pump's own password.

Check it: run npm run automation:water-ingress -- --headed against a live login and look at whether the Enter Details grid shows one outlet or an outlet picker.

BlockerDoes the portal really kick the older session?

The whole locking design rests on "a second login kicks the first". That belief appears in two comments and is handled nowhere in the code — the login classifier has no wording for it at all.

Check it: open a second browser on the same username while the first sits on the grid, press Save in the first, and write down exactly what the portal says.

What could bite

Ranked by how likely it is to actually happen

HighOne long batch can make another pump miss its window

The server runs one browser at a time and serves whoever queued first — no priorities. A 15-minute batch for pump A parks pump B's water check behind it, and B's check has a legal deadline that A's batch does not. A missed window cannot be back-filled, ever.

Fix: cap a batch at 14 minutes, and let a job with a closing window jump the queue. That queue-jump is about 20 lines and is worth doing even if batching never ships.

HighPulling a job earlier makes it walk earlier every day

A daily job dragged 37 minutes early to join a batch is rescheduled from when it finished, not from when it was due. So tomorrow it starts 37 minutes early again, and again. In a month it has circled the clock.

Fix: reschedule from the slot the job was supposed to occupy, not from the clock. Three lines — and they must land in the same commit as the first pull-forward, not after.

HighTwo services rarely come due together by luck

A 6-hourly job lands within 15 minutes of a 2-hourly one about one time in eight. So almost all the saving has to come from moving a job earlier — and moving it changes what it means. A credit reading taken 90 minutes early is a different reading.

Fix: every service must state in writing how far it may be pulled forward. No global default, no inheriting — the service owner declares it or it never moves.

MediumNothing tells anyone when a run fails

There is no alert, email or push anywhere — a failure is a log line and a row in the database. Making one failure cost three services at once, with nobody watching, is how a pump quietly stops being served.

Fix: ship a daily digest of failed runs before the batch. It is useful on its own and it is the price of admission for making failures blast wider.

MediumThe savings claim is partly already banked

The "45 seconds of forced waiting between logins" only gets paid when jobs run back-to-back. TT density was deliberately moved to 07:35, well clear of the 07:00 water rush, so today it waits zero. Do not delete that hand-tuning to prove the new mechanism works.

Fix: drop the 45-second saving from the business case, and leave 07:35 alone until the batch has run clean for a fortnight.

MediumGood news: a half-finished batch is already safe

If a batch dies halfway, nothing is corrupted and nothing needs replaying — the portal's own page is the record of what was done. Water ingress reads the portal's "Updated On" timestamp; density is keyed on the invoice number. Do not build resume or checkpoint machinery. The next cycle is the retry.

One catch: create each service's run record the moment its errand starts, never all of them up front, or a dead batch leaves ghost rows claiming to be running.

The one rule

If you remember nothing else

Never delay a job to build a batch.
Only pull one forward.

Water ingress is always the anchor and never a passenger. If a batch ever pushes a water check past its window, a truthful compliance record becomes a permanently missed one — and there is no way to fix it after.

Pull the density check 20 min early → joins the batch Hold the water check 5 min late → waits for a friend

How the industry does this

Nothing here is new — it all has a name and a precedent

OpenSSH · ControlMaster

One handshake, many sessions

Exactly this shape: pay for the expensive authentication once, run many cheap logical sessions over it. And close it the moment the last one leaves — an idle session here would hold the box's only browser.

Kubernetes · PriorityClass

Deadlines beat arrival order

The missing piece. Our browser queue is strictly first-come-first-served, so a hard-deadline water check cannot overtake a soft-deadline batch. This is the single highest-value change in the whole idea.

Stripe · Idempotency-Key

The other side owns the receipt

Already in place and load-bearing: the portal's own timestamp is the proof, never the radio button we just clicked. This is why a half-done batch needs no repair.

AWS RDS Proxy · pinning

Some sessions are not shareable

When an admin presses "Run now", they get a private session nobody may join — so a run they are watching stays clean and debuggable.

HikariCP · connection eviction

Never hand out a poisoned session

The code already recognises "bounced back to the login page". A shared session needs a version stamp so a broken one is retired instead of passed to the next errand.

Linux · interrupt coalescing

Batch by advancing, never by waiting

The rule above, in its industrial form. Group work that was going to happen anyway; never create a batch by holding something back.

What to build, in order

Each step is useful alone · you may stop after any of them

  1. Time a login

    One line of code. Record how many seconds a portal login takes, then read twenty real runs. If it turns out to be 40 seconds rather than three minutes, batching is not worth its shared-failure risk and you stop after step 3.

    1 line · settles the whole business case

  2. Answer the two open questions in a live browser

    One outlet per login or several; and what the portal really does with a second session. Both change the design.

    one afternoon · no code

  3. Add failure alerts

    A daily digest of failed runs into the admin screen. Nothing warns anyone today, and that must be fixed before failures are allowed to get bigger.

    small · useful on its own

  4. Extract the shared session — this is the real prize

    One "open a portal session" object that all four services use, replacing four hand-kept copies of the same block. Behaviour identical to today. This alone is what makes the fifth service cheap to add — batching or not.

    medium · zero behaviour change · stop here if step 1 disappoints

  5. Let a closing deadline jump the browser queue

    So a water check with 20 minutes of window left overtakes a job that could run any time today.

    ~20 lines · worth it even if batching never ships

  6. Then, and only then: the batch itself

    Off by default behind a switch. Same pump only. Water ingress always the anchor, at most two passengers, 14-minute cap. Turn it on for one pump, watch for a fortnight, then widen.

    the switch is also the rollback

What this does not fix

Say it out loud so nobody assumes otherwise

All the locking that keeps two sessions off one portal account lives inside a single running copy of the backend. A second copy, or a script run by hand over SSH, sees none of it. Batching sits on top of that hole and does not close it. Closing it means moving the lock into the database — separate work, on its own merits, not smuggled into this project.

And batching saves no memory. The peak is still one browser; you are simply holding it open four times longer on a box that has already been killed for running out of memory.

Every figure above was read out of the running code, not estimated — except the length of a login, which has never been measured. That is why measuring it is step one.
